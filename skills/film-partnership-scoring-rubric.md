# Film Partnership Scoring Rubric

## How to use this rubric

For each film on the slate, score all ten dimensions independently.
Base each score only on the criteria and anchors defined below.
Do not let a high score on one dimension inflate another.
After scoring all ten, sum the scores. Flag low confidence where specified.
Return: the total score, each dimension score, and one plain-language
reason sentence per dimension.

---

## Dimension 1: Audience Reach and Demographic Fit
**What this measures:** Whether the film's core audience matches the
brand's target consumer base of families and adults aged 18-49, with
bonus weight for Gen Z crossover and global reach.

**Why it matters:** A partnership only drives awareness and sales lift
if the brand's buyers are actually in the theater or watching the content.
Narrow or mismatched audiences mean the spend reaches the wrong people.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Broad 4-quadrant audience (men, women, families, multiple age groups). Global IP with documented cross-generational reach. Gen Z engagement confirmed. Example: Avengers: Doomsday, Frozen 3. |
| 7-8 | Strong primary audience match (families or 18-49 adults) with partial Gen Z crossover. Example: Spider-Man: Brand New Day, Cat in the Hat. |
| 5-6 | Solid but narrower audience. Reaches the brand's core but misses one key segment. Example: Focker In-Law (adult-skewing, limited family appeal). |
| 3-4 | Niche or mismatched audience. Example: The Thomas Crown Affair (prestige adult, 35-55 skew, limited mass-market overlap). |
| 1-2 | Audience is too narrow or too young to drive meaningful brand lift. Example: Cocomelon the Movie (toddler/preschool only). |

## Dimension 2: Brand Alignment and Safety
**What this measures:** Whether the film's tone, IP, story themes, and
cultural associations protect and extend the brand's equity. Covers both
brand safety (does this film carry reputational risk) and brand fit
(does this film reinforce what the brand stands for).

**Why it matters:** A brand that partners with a tonally wrong property
creates dissonance in the consumer's mind. The partnership has to feel
inevitable, not forced. Brand safety is non-negotiable: a single
controversy association can take years to undo.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Warm, family-friendly, aspirational IP with nostalgia equity that maps directly to the brand's heritage and positioning. No brand safety risk. Example: Frozen 3, Cat in the Hat. |
| 7-8 | Strong thematic alignment with minor tone considerations. Broad-appeal IP with a clear brand story connection. Example: Avengers: Doomsday (heroic, aspirational, family-adjacent). |
| 5-6 | Reasonable fit with one area of tension. The brand story connection works but requires a creative bridge. Example: K-Pop Superstar (culturally aligned but unfamiliar IP territory for a legacy CPG brand). |
| 3-4 | Tonal mismatch or brand safety flag. Dark, edgy, or franchise-adjacent content that creates dissonance with a warm CPG brand. Example: The Batman: Part II (noir crime thriller, not family-forward). |
| 1-2 | Clear brand safety risk or fundamental positioning conflict. The association would confuse or alienate the brand's core consumer. |

## Dimension 3: Cultural Moment and Earned Media Potential
**What this measures:** Whether this film is a genuine cultural event
that will generate social conversation, press coverage, and earned media
beyond its paid marketing — or whether it is simply a theatrical release.

**Why it matters:** A brand partnership with a true cultural tentpole
gets amplified by the film's own marketing machine and the organic
conversation it creates. A partnership with a mid-tier release requires
the brand to do all the heavy lifting to generate attention.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Major cultural event. Sustained press momentum, high social conversation volume, Gen Z fandom engagement, and a marketing campaign the brand can ride. Example: Avengers: Secret Wars (MCU finale), Frozen 3 (8-year-anticipated sequel). |
| 7-8 | Strong cultural footprint. Clear fandom base and press moment, even if not a once-in-a-decade event. Example: Spider-Man: Brand New Day, Superman: Man of Tomorrow. |
| 5-6 | Moderate cultural presence. Has an audience and some press, but the brand must generate most of its own momentum. Example: K-Pop Superstar (niche cultural moment, strong within its community). |
| 3-4 | Limited cultural footprint. Mid-tier release with low organic conversation potential. Example: The Thomas Crown Affair. |
| 1-2 | Minimal cultural presence. Unlikely to generate earned media at scale. |

## Dimension 4: Activation Runway and Timing
**What this measures:** How much lead time exists between today and the
film's release date for the brand to develop, produce, and launch
co-promotional assets. Longer runway enables higher-value activations.

**Why it matters:** Co-branded limited-edition product (the highest-value
CPG partnership asset) requires a minimum of 18 months from brief to
shelf. Limited-edition packaging requires 9 to 12 months. Social and
experiential activations can close in 90 days. A film that releases in
6 months can only support amplification. A film that releases in 24
months can support a full product launch.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | 12 to 18 months to release. Full co-branded product development possible (limited-edition SKU, custom flavor, co-branded packaging line). Highest activation ceiling. Example: Avengers: Secret Wars (Dec 2027), Frozen 3 (Nov 2027). |
| 7-8 | 6 to 12 months to release. Limited-edition packaging and co-branded content possible. Product development is achievable at pace — a well-resourced brand can execute an LTO gift box in this window. Example: Most mid-2027 titles evaluated from mid-2026. |
| 5-6 | 6 to 9 months to release. Social campaign, experiential activation, and packaging updates possible. New product development is unlikely but not impossible with a streamlined brand approval process. |
| 3-4 | 3 to 6 months to release. Amplification and in-market social only. No product or packaging changes possible in this window. Example: Cat in the Hat (Nov 2026), Focker In-Law (Nov 2026). |
| 1-2 | Under 3 months to release. Limited brand activation possible: a co-sponsored screening, limited social promotions, and in-market amplification of the studio's existing campaign. Example: Spider-Man: Brand New Day (July 2026). |

**Note:** Score based on months from the date this rubric is applied
(June 2026) to the film's confirmed release date.

## Dimension 5: Integration Potential
**What this measures:** Whether the brand can be embedded inside the
film's actual content — through product placement, a named brand
presence in a scene, or co-branded content that features the film's
characters or world. Distinct from the marketing campaign around the film.

**Why it matters:** In-film integration delivers impression volume that
paid media cannot replicate and carries implicit endorsement from the
film's creative. It is the highest-value placement but is structurally
impossible in some film types regardless of budget.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Live-action theatrical with confirmed precedent for brand integration in the franchise or from the director. Brand placement is natural to the film's world. Example: Untitled Apatow/Powell film (adult comedy, real-world setting, strong integration precedent from Apatow's prior work). |
| 7-8 | Live-action theatrical where integration is possible and precedented in the genre, but not yet confirmed. Example: Focker In-Law, K-Pop Superstar. |
| 5-6 | Integration is possible but limited or sparse given the film's world or tone. Superhero tentpoles have documented precedent for brand integration (e.g. Lexus in Black Panther) but these require deep storyline alignment rather than incidental product placement — they are not brand-soup films. Example: The Thomas Crown Affair (original 1968, remake 1999; any comp data requires significant assumptions given the age of source material). |
| 3-4 | Integration is structurally constrained. Dark or grounded IP where a consumer brand presence would break the world. Example: The Batman: Part II. |
| 1-2 | Integration is structurally impossible. Animated features (brand logos cannot exist in the film's world) or fantasy IP where the setting precludes real-world brand presence. Example: Frozen 3, The Lord of the Rings: The Hunt for Gollum, Cocomelon the Movie. |

**Important:** A low integration score does not disqualify a title.
It creates tension with other high scores that the human gate must resolve.
Frozen 3 scoring 1 on integration and 10 on audience reach is the
correct output. The human decides whether the co-promotional upside
outweighs the absence of in-film presence.

## Dimension 6: Franchise Activation Precedent
**What this measures:** Whether clean, franchise-specific comp data exists
to support the partnership case — and what that comp history implies for
the activation ceiling. This dimension scores the quality and depth of
available evidence, not the ACC in-film ROI calculation (which is produced
separately by the ROI sub-agent using the proprietary exposure formula).

**Why it matters:** The number the agent produces is only as trustworthy
as the data underneath it. A scored partnership built on genre-level
guesses is a different risk than one grounded in documented franchise
comp data. This dimension makes that distinction visible and scoreable,
and it is the one most exposed to hallucination risk — so it is grounded
in a structured comp lookup, not the model's opinion. If comp data is
unavailable, the score is capped at 5 and a low-confidence flag is returned.

**What this dimension does not measure:** The actual dollar value of in-film
integration. That calculation uses the ACC proprietary formula and is
produced by the ROI sub-agent running in parallel at the orchestrator level.
The two outputs serve different purposes: this dimension flags data confidence
for the scoring chain; the ROI sub-agent produces the client-deliverable figure.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Clean franchise-specific comp data available. Prior CPG partnerships with this IP or direct franchise precedent are documented and retrievable. High confidence in the activation ceiling. Example: Frozen 3 (Frozen 1: $1.28B global, Frozen 2: $1.45B global; prior Disney animated franchise CPG partnerships well-documented). A Minecraft Movie Sequel (first film: $900M+ global, prior CPG partner data available). |
| 7-8 | Strong comp data with one area of uncertainty. Franchise history supports the case but a single variable — schedule risk, platform split, or MCU saturation — reduces confidence. Example: Avengers franchise (well-documented CPG lift, but saturation of MCU partnerships is a real comp-distortion factor). |
| 5-6 | Partial comp data. Directionally defensible but requires assumptions to fill gaps. Example: Superman: Man of Tomorrow (prior Superman partnership comps exist but the DCU franchise reset introduces uncertainty about applicability). |
| 3-4 | Thin comp data. Estimate relies on genre-level comparables rather than franchise-specific data. Flag for human review. Example: K-Pop Superstar (no direct franchise comp; genre-level music film partnership data used as proxy). |
| 1-2 | No reliable comp data. Release format unconfirmed, schedule at risk, or streaming-only window eliminates theatrical activation precedent. Return a low-confidence flag. Example: Narnia: The Magician's Nephew (Netflix; no public theatrical comp data available). |

**Low-confidence flag trigger:** Return flag if franchise-specific comp data
is unavailable, if the release date is confirmed at risk (e.g., omitted from
studio marketing materials), or if the title has no confirmed release format
(theatrical vs. streaming).

## Dimension 7: Talent Alignment and Promotional Commitment
**What this measures:** Whether the film's above-the-line talent
(director and lead cast) has documented brand equity, owned social
reach, and a track record of showing up for promotional partners —
not just the film's marketing campaign.

**Why it matters:** Co-branded content requires a human face. A brand
partnership where the talent does not engage leaves the brand holding
a logo placement. The PR and talent partnership leads evaluate this
before any deal closes. A film with anonymous ensemble cast or talent
with limited social presence scores low regardless of the film's
other attributes.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Lead talent has large owned platform, documented history of brand partnership engagement, and a coherent brand-talent story connection. Example: Glen Powell (Untitled Apatow film) — strong social presence, proven brand partner, aspirational consumer profile. Ariana Grande — global fanbase, documented brand partnership history, cross-generational reach. |
| 7-8 | Lead talent is culturally prominent with strong social reach, even if brand partnership history is limited. Film has a clear talent face, not an ensemble. Example: Michael B. Jordan (The Thomas Crown Affair) — strong cultural profile, aspirational consumer association. |
| 5-6 | Ensemble cast with no single dominant talent face, or a property where the most recognizable talent plays a supporting or minor role rather than the lead. Example: Narnia: The Magician's Nephew, where recognizable talent may not carry the promotional campaign. |
| 3-4 | Talent is prominent in-film but largely unavailable or unlikely to engage in co-promotional content (scheduling, contractual limits, or brand mismatch). Example: Voice-only animated talent (Frozen 3 cast is familiar but animated partnership content is limited in format). |
| 1-2 | No identifiable talent face for co-promotional content. Motion-capture or fully animated title where talent presence is structurally absent from marketing assets. Example: Cocomelon the Movie. |

## Dimension 8: Relationship Fostering Opportunity
**What this measures:** Whether this partnership opens or deepens relationships that compound over time — with the studio, but also with the film's director, producers, and A-list lead talent, all of whom hold approval rights over promotional campaign elements. A great campaign does not just earn the next studio deal; it earns trust with the creative stakeholders who decide whether a brand is a worthy partner for their work.

**Why it matters:** Directors, producers, and talent experience a brand's workflow directly through the partnership. A brand that brings campaign elements that do not speak to the film's audience, misses deadlines, or pushes activations that feel inauthentic leaves a poor impression on the studio, ATL team, and talent alike — closing doors on future partnerships regardless of budget. A brand that demonstrates it truly understands the film's world rises to the top of the consideration list for the next project. The relationship fostered here extends beyond a legal deal to a creative reputation.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Partnership opens access to a major studio franchise ecosystem with multiple upcoming relevant titles AND the film's director, producer, and lead talent are known to be collaborative, brand-positive, and likely to become long-term advocates if the campaign work is excellent. Example: Any WB/DC title (access to Superman, Batman, LOTR, and future DC slate); any Marvel title (access to the full MCU pipeline). Russo Brothers on Secret Wars have a strong brand partnership history. |
| 7-8 | Partnership deepens an existing studio relationship or opens a studio with a strong near-term slate, and the ATL creative team has a documented track record of authentic brand collaboration. |
| 5-6 | Moderate relationship value. Studio has some relevant upcoming titles. ATL stakeholder relationship is neutral — the campaign work matters but there is no strong prior signal of collaborative brand partnership culture from the director or lead talent. |
| 3-4 | Standalone title with limited franchise extension. Studio relationship has low near-term follow-on value. ATL creative stakeholders are unlikely to carry the relationship forward. Example: Thomas Crown Affair (MGM; limited franchise pipeline relevant to this brand). |
| 1-2 | One-off title with no franchise extension, no studio relationship value, and ATL stakeholders who are either unavailable for partnership collaboration or have a track record of limiting brand involvement in their creative work. |

## Dimension 9: Key Market Activation Fit
**What this measures:** Whether the film's release footprint, premiere cities, and studio marketing infrastructure align with the brand's priority retail markets — domestically and globally. Most partnership campaigns target global impact, not domestic reach alone. A title with both a US and international premiere unlocks two distinct activation windows and two distinct earned media spikes.

**Why it matters:** A partnership is only as valuable as the brand's ability to show up in the markets where it matters. A global tentpole with premieres in NYC and London gives a brand two high-profile on-the-ground moments and signals the studio's commitment to international marketing. A US-only release or a streaming drop without a theatrical premiere window limits the activation architecture. The events and experiential lead evaluates premiere access, market footprint, and the brand's capacity to activate on the ground in each market.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | Film has both a major US premiere (NYC or LA) and a major international premiere (London, Tokyo, Paris, or equivalent) — reflecting genuine global audience and studio commitment to multi-market marketing. Brand can embed in both premiere moments. Example: The Lord of the Rings: The Hunt for Gollum (global audience with strong UK/NZ/European market; multiple international premieres expected). Avengers: Secret Wars (global MCU audience; dual-market premiere infrastructure confirmed by franchise precedent). |
| 7-8 | Strong US premiere in a key brand market with partial international footprint. One anchor premiere event with secondary global activation possible. Example: Spider-Man: Brand New Day, Frozen 3. |
| 5-6 | National US release with standard studio co-promotional package. Single premiere city, domestic focus. Untitled Apatow/Powell film likely falls here — US-targeted country music and comedy premise, premiere likely in LA or Nashville with limited international premiere rationale. |
| 3-4 | Limited market activation architecture. Streaming-first title with a split theatrical and streaming window that divides the studio's marketing effort between driving theatrical attendance and driving streaming subscriptions. Example: Narnia: The Magician's Nephew (Netflix) — likely has a global premiere given the IP's international reach, but the 2/12 theatrical to 4/2 Netflix transition splits the marketing push and reduces the brand's ability to anchor to a single clear audience moment. |
| 1-2 | No market activation path. Streaming-only drop with no theatrical premiere, no red-carpet infrastructure, and no physical retail or event tie-in available. |

## Dimension 10: Quality and Creative Potential
**What this measures:** How strong and coherent the brand-film
narrative connection is for co-branded content. Specifically: is
there a clear, authentic story that puts the brand and the film
in the same sentence without forcing it?

**Why it matters:** The creative lead and brand team must believe
in the story before committing budget. A partnership where the
brand-film connection is contrived produces weak content that
neither the studio nor the brand will amplify. A partnership with
a natural narrative hook produces content both sides want to push.

**Scoring anchors:**

| Score | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| 9-10 | The brand-film story connection is immediate, authentic, and requires no creative forcing. The brand's values, history, or product naturally lives inside the film's world or themes. Example: A family CPG brand with Frozen 3 (family, the next generation, and the film's anticipated themes around new beginnings and a baby — matching a brand's own story of legacy and the next chapter); a snack brand with Avengers (energy, fuel, the team). |
| 7-8 | Strong narrative connection that requires a single creative bridge but holds up under scrutiny. The story is coherent and both the brand and studio would be proud to amplify it. |
| 5-6 | Workable creative connection that requires more development. The brand and film can share a campaign but the story is not immediately obvious to a consumer. |
| 3-4 | Weak narrative connection. The brand and film can co-exist in marketing but the creative team will struggle to tell a story that feels earned. |
| 1-2 | No coherent brand-film narrative. Any co-branded content would feel like a logo placement exercise. The creative team would not recommend proceeding on story grounds alone. |

## Consistency rule

The same title scored twice with the same input data must return
the same score on every dimension. Variance of more than 1 point
on any dimension across runs is a consistency failure and triggers
a low-confidence flag on that dimension.

## What this rubric does not do

- It does not recommend whether to pursue a partnership. That is
  the human gate's job.
- It does not account for relationship history, internal budget
  constraints, or brand-specific strategic priorities that are not
  encoded here. Those belong at the human gate.
- It does not score titles that are not on the confirmed slate.
